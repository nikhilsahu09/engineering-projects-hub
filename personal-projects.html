PROJECT 01 · SMART PLANT CARE

AquaBalance

An automatic plant-watering prototype designed to help prevent plants from being under-watered or over-watered.

What is AquaBalance?

Remembering to water plants can be difficult: forgetting to water or adding too much can harm them. AquaBalance is an automatic plant-watering machine built around a custom 3D-printed vase designed to help contain water and support a plant.

The prototype also served as a way to explore electronics, sensor readings, pump control, and embedded coding in a practical home-gardening application.

Why I chose this project

The project offered a hands-on way to learn how a soil-moisture sensor works, how to control a submersible pump, and how to display sensor readings on a 7-segment LED. It also connected to an everyday interest in gardening and the possibility of scaling automatic watering to a larger setup.

SYSTEM DESIGN

How it works

01

Measure soil moisture

An analog soil-moisture sensor reads the soil condition and provides a value to the Arduino, with raw readings typically in the 0–1023 range.

02

Process the reading

The Arduino code reads the sensor and scales the reading to a simpler 0–100 display for the 7-segment LED.

03

Water when needed

When the soil is considered dry, the Arduino activates a mini submersible pump. Water moves through tubing in intervals until the target moisture condition is reached.

04

Contain and deliver water

A custom-designed 3D-printed vase and carefully routed tubing help manage the water delivery and reduce leakage or siphoning issues.

COMPONENTS

Electronics & fabrication

Main components

  • Arduino Uno
  • Analog soil-moisture sensor
  • Mini submersible water pump and plastic tubing
  • 7-segment LED
  • Jumper wires, NPN transistor, resistor, and flyback diode
  • Water reservoir and custom 3D-printed vase

CAD & MAKING

Custom 3D-printed vase

The vase was designed in Autodesk Inventor and printed for the prototype. The total filament used was approximately 297 grams. At a filament cost of $30 per kilogram, the estimated print-material cost was $8.91.

BUILD PROCESS

Construction overview

01

Design the vase

Create a custom vase in Autodesk Inventor and 3D-print it. If the print cannot be completed in time, use a suitable container as a backup.

02

Prepare pump wiring

Attach secure leads to the pump terminals and insulate the connections. Use appropriate adult supervision and safe soldering practices.

03

Test each component

Test the 7-segment display, soil-moisture sensor, and pump individually before combining them. Ensure the pump has water when operating.

04

Connect the water path

Attach tubing to the pump and position the outlet above the soil. Keep the tubing end out of the soil to help prevent dirt entering the pump.

05

Wire and program the system

Connect the moisture sensor to an analog input, the display to its required interface, and the pump through a transistor switching circuit with a flyback diode. Write and debug the control code.

06

Test for leaks and siphoning

Check wiring, verify the code, and test the vase and tubing for leakage or unintended water flow.

Problems & solutions

  • Incomplete 3D print: used a plastic container as a backup reservoir.
  • Thin vase bottom: had the vase reprinted.
  • Faulty LCD panels: switched to a 7-segment LED to display moisture on a 0–100 scale.
  • Water leakage: added a rubber-like lining inside the vase.
  • Pump would not start: checked component seating, wiring, and transistor pin configuration.

Reflection

This project introduced me to several unfamiliar components and challenged me to troubleshoot both hardware and code. I learned how soil-moisture readings are gathered, how analog inputs are used, why a transistor is needed to switch the pump, and why a flyback diode matters in a motor circuit. Testing each component separately and checking the wiring carefully were important steps toward getting the system working.

PROJECT 02 · ROBOTICS + MECHATRONICS

Automated Desk Drawer

A robotics/mechatronics capstone project combining fingerprint access, autonomous sensing, a motorized drawer, and a ball-launching mechanism.

Objective

Create a robotics/mechatronics product that demonstrates skills developed over two years of classes. The design used buttons and sensors rather than wireless operation, required at least two sensors in a feedback loop, and included at least one student-designed 3D-printed part, along with planning and a final presentation.

Project concept

The drawer uses a fingerprint sensor to authorize access. A VEX rangefinder detects a nearby hand and commands the drawer to open through a horizontal rack-and-pinion system. A bumper switch closes the drawer. A ball launcher was included as the project's intruder-response demonstration.

FINAL PRODUCT

Three integrated systems

01

Drawer and frame

VEX metal angles, C-channels, plates, screws, and nuts form the base and drawer. A horizontal rack-and-pinion mechanism driven by a VEX motor group moves the drawer in and out.

02

Fingerprint access

A DY50 fingerprint sensor connects to an Arduino and uses the Adafruit fingerprint library to identify a stored fingerprint. The Arduino sends a signal to the VEX brain to select the appropriate response.

03

Ball launcher

A VEX motor group drives two wheels that compress and launch foam balls. A custom 3D-printed holder was designed in Autodesk Inventor to hold the balls above the launcher.

BUILD & CONTROL

Design and implementation

01

Build the base

I initially considered a fully 3D-printed base, but the long print time and material durability made that approach impractical. VEX metal parts were used for the main frame instead.

02

Construct the drawer slide

Build the drawer from VEX angles and plates, then attach the rack-and-pinion slide and motor group to move it forward and backward.

03

Connect fingerprint recognition

Wire the DY50 fingerprint sensor to the Arduino and use the Adafruit library to enroll and recognize a valid fingerprint.

04

Link Arduino and VEX

The Arduino sends a digital signal through a VEX 3-wire connection. The VEX program interprets the signal, then uses the rangefinder and bumper switch to control drawer motion.

05

Design and attach the ball holder

Model a holder for two VEX foam balls in Autodesk Inventor, print it, and mount it above the launcher mechanism.

06

Program and test

Run the Arduino C++ fingerprint code alongside the VEX Python control code. Test valid and invalid scans, range detection, drawer opening and closing, and the launcher response.

Challenges & solutions

  • Base print took too long: changed to VEX metal parts.
  • Drawer sagged: moved support points toward the middle to better distribute the load.
  • Fingerprint readings were unreliable: used the Adafruit library's enrollment and matching approach.
  • Ball holder mounting was unstable: used tape as a quick fix, though a more secure mounting method would have been better.

Successes & reflection

I adapted the design when the original approach proved impractical, planned my work using a daily calendar, and completed the project within the class timeline. The most challenging part was connecting Arduino and VEX code so the fingerprint result could trigger the correct mechanical behavior. The build strengthened my skills in prototyping, mechatronics, debugging, and making design trade-offs under time constraints.
