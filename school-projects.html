PROJECT 01 · AUTONOMOUS ROBOTICS

Cargo Transport

An autonomous robot challenge focused on moving colored cargo cubes to receiving docks, balancing mechanical reliability, sensing, scoring strategy, and limited build time.

Challenge brief

The robot had to transport at least three custom 3D-printed colored cubes to docks without human interaction after the autonomous start. A round lasted three minutes, and the average score across three rounds determined performance.

12Build days
3 minPer round
3Rounds averaged
6Blocks in programmed cycle

Rules and constraints

  • Blocks could not touch the table while being scored.
  • The pickup-to-goal distance was 24 inches, adjustable by about ±3 inches.
  • Pickup heights were 0.5–2 inches and goal heights were 1–4 inches.
  • Only VEX parts could be used, except custom parts designed and 3D-printed by the team.
  • The robot had to operate autonomously. Moving the shipping boat or receiving dock forfeited the round.

Scoring strategy

Dock locationMultiplierYellow blockRed blockBlue block
Outside platform1×2 pts4 pts6 pts
Top platform1.5×2 pts4 pts6 pts
Cave2×2 pts4 pts6 pts

The multiplier is applied according to the block's scoring destination. Sensor utilization, design, functionality, number of blocks picked up, aesthetics, wire management, construction, and planning/concept production were also part of evaluation.

Design evolution

Initial concept

Base and rotating arm

The first concept used a rectangular, SquareBot-like base with wheels and motors. A metal plate and turntable bearing supported an arm driven through gearing and a Motor 393.

Redesign

Stability and height

The wheel-based approach was discarded. Angles were used to improve height and stability, and two smart motors were grouped to improve control precision.

Sensing

Claw and optical sensor

A claw picked up the cubes, while an optical sensor identified their colors. Screws and collars were tightened during refinement.

Autonomous code logic

  1. The robot begins at its starting position and rotates the arm about 180 degrees to the opposite side.
  2. The optical sensor reads the cube color.
  3. Blue sends the block to the cave, red to the top platform, and other/yellow blocks to the outside platform.
  4. The cycle repeats for the planned set of six blocks. Returning to the base between pickups was necessary because the block positions were planned.

Testing and troubleshooting

Arm power and weight

The turntable bearing lacked sufficient power for the heavy arm, and the arm could drop or crash downward. Adjustments helped, but the issue was not fully resolved.

First pickup

The first attempt failed. Repeated troubleshooting and iterative adjustments eventually allowed the robot to pick up its first block.

Position consistency

Variation in robot and block positioning made repeatability difficult. The design relied on planned positions, limiting flexibility.

Time constraints

Two build days were disrupted, leaving little time to finish. The experience highlighted the importance of realistic scheduling and prioritizing critical functions.

Cost management

The project tracked VEX parts and estimated costs for working hours/pay, electronics, metal, and plastic parts. Use the original class price list for the exact itemized cost record.

Reflection

This project reinforced that a robot can have a promising concept but still struggle when the mechanism is too heavy or not powerful enough. Iterative testing, time management, listening to teammates, and being willing to reconsider ideas were important. I focused especially on coding and documenting the process, and learned that failure is part of improving a design.

PROJECT 02 · AUTOMATION SYSTEMS

Pizza Automation

A recurring, rule-based process designed to make a pizza through several automated stages without human intervention during the sequence.

Project requirements

The system needed three changes and four different pizza models (one per stage), at least one tactile sensor and one optical sensor, 3D-printed base models, and VEX metal parts and VEX V5 coding. Human interaction and unsafe wiring could incur deductions; cost was also a consideration.

Concept selection

The team brainstormed and sketched concepts including a pizza, a snowman, and a table. A decision matrix was used to compare options, and the pizza process was selected.

Four product stages

01

Crusted pizza

The base model enters the automated line.

02

Sauced pizza

A claw mechanism squeezes a sauce bottle.

03

Cheese + pepperoni

A custom cup tips toppings onto the pizza.

04

Sliced pizza

A four-blade slicer descends and rises using a vertical rack-and-pinion mechanism.

How the system works

  1. The process begins when the start button is pressed. The conveyor moves until a distance sensor detects the pizza, then stops.
  2. A claw mechanism squeezes the sauce bottle over the pizza.
  3. The next button advances the conveyor until another sensor detects the pizza, then the cheese-and-pepperoni cup tips.
  4. After the next process is triggered by a leaf switch, the slicer moves down and back up using the rack and pinion.
  5. The final start button moves the pizza off the conveyor and into a paper pizza box; the program then terminates.

My contributions

3D modeling and printing

I modeled the pizza products for the different stages and printed them. The four models made the product's progress visible through the process.

Slicer mechanism

I worked on the slicer and researched a vertical rack-and-pinion mechanism. I learned from teammates and online demonstrations as we developed the mechanism.

Packaging

I made a custom paper pizza box for the finished product to land in at the end of the conveyor.

Distinctive parts included the rack and pinion, conveyor belt, bumper switch, and leaf switch. The team aimed to balance cost, performance, clean construction, and dependable operation. The topping dropper worked, and the final design was relatively cost-efficient compared with other groups.

Reflection

The project showed how important it is to integrate mechanical systems and code carefully. Sensor placement, belt tension, switch contact, gear alignment, and conveyor speed all affected whether each stage worked. The team split tasks, used a Gantt chart for long-term planning, and had to spend time integrating and troubleshooting the code and mechanisms.
